  • Discount pricing plus the customer draft (#103) should be one agent. The 10% vs 20% conflict happened because two separate Accounting runs couldn't see each other, and Customer Service wrote its draft with the first answer. One agent holding the check_stock_and_pricing result, the cash balance and the draft in the same context would give one price and one matching email. It would also skip the 3 repeated MCP calls.
  • Customer Service barely used its own tools. On both tickets it worked (#101 and #103) it only called get_ticket. Everything it wrote came from facts other agents passed in. Whoever already holds those facts could write the draft.
  • The Boss is mostly overhead on simple tickets. Re-reading reports cost 52% of #101's tokens. For one-topic tickets like rent, a simple rule ("rent_notice → rent agent") would do the routing without a model.
  • Where multiple agents did earn their keep:
    • Least privilege: Customer Service never sees cash or invoices, so a draft can't leak "we're $112 short".
    • Separation: the agents that prepare money are never the ones that pay it.
    • Auditability: the audit trail shows exactly which role read which number.
    • Cross-checking: on #103 the Boss caught Accounting and Customer Service disagreeing, which one agent might have glossed over.
    I would keep that split for money and customer messages, and merge everything else.

7. What I learned building and testing it

  • Prompts need scope, not just rules. In the first test of #102 (Problem 5), Accounting opened all three tickets and prepared invoice 501 and a tee order on a rent ticket, because Facilities told it to check cash "against everything else waiting". One sentence in the Accounting prompt ("stay on the ticket you were given") fixed it, and in the final run Accounting stayed on the rent.
  • Safety rules belong in code, not just in prompts. The prompts say "never pay". What actually guaranteed it was hiding the pay tools from agents, making the tools refuse non-human approvers and negative balances, and disabling Approve on the board. On #103 those code checks did the work: Accounting still prepared a $264 order the shop couldn't afford, and the code stopped it from going through.
  • Full connectivity is powerful but needs limits. Customer Service delegating to Accounting was actually smart (it needed a price). The cost was a second, inconsistent Accounting answer. The guardrails we set never fired (no loops, chains stayed within 3 agents deep, no ticket hit 6 delegations), but there was no rule against two runs of the same agent answering the same question.
  • An audit trail per step is what made this reflection possible. Every number above (who called what, 18,624 tokens for Accounting on #103, the two discount answers) comes from audit_trail.json, not from what the agents said about themselves.
